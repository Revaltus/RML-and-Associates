Most nonprofit budgets get scrutiny on the big lines: salaries, rent, program costs. The $29 here and $75 there rarely get a second look. That is exactly where the leak is. A donor database nobody opened since the last board turnover, a video conferencing plan with six seats and two users, a design tool that renewed for a full year on a card the former office manager used. Each one is small. Together they behave like a recurring expense that was never approved.

Subscription creep is what happens when monthly and annual charges pile up faster than anyone reviews them. For a Laramie nonprofit running on grants, membership dues and a few good fundraisers, it deserves the same discipline as your insurance, your utilities or your payroll-sized commitments: a line in the budget, an owner, and a date when someone looks at it again.

## Why recurring charges deserve a budget line

A one-time purchase goes through a decision. Somebody asks, somebody approves, somebody writes the check. A subscription skips that step after the first month. It renews on its own, quietly, and the charge lands in the ledger under a vague vendor name that nobody connects to a program.

Here is an illustrative example. Say a Laramie nonprofit with a $150,000 annual budget carries these recurring charges:

| Subscription | Monthly | Annual | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| Donor database | $129 | $1,548 | Are we using the tier we pay for? |
| Email marketing | $75 | $900 | Is the list size still driving the price? |
| Video conferencing (4 licenses) | $64 | $768 | How many people logged in last quarter? |
| Design software | $30 | $360 | Does a free nonprofit plan exist? |
| E-signature | $25 | $300 | How many documents did we send? |
| Cloud storage | $60 | $720 | Do we have two storage accounts? |
| Online giving add-on | $49 | $588 | Is it duplicating a platform feature? |
| Volunteer scheduling app | $40 | $480 | Who still uses this? |
| **Total** | **$472** | **$5,664** | |

That is close to 4 percent of the budget, committed without a single vote. Trim even three of those lines and you free up roughly $1,800 a year for something your mission can actually point to, like a workshop series, a program supply order or a reserve contribution.

## Where subscriptions hide in a nonprofit

Nonprofits have a few habits that make creep worse than it is at a typical business.

**Staff and volunteer turnover.** When a bookkeeper or program coordinator leaves, their logins, saved cards and renewal emails often go with them. Charges keep coming, sometimes to an email address nobody checks. A departing staff member is one of the most common triggers we see for a clean-up, and it is much easier to do on purpose than in a panic.

**Board turnover.** New treasurers inherit a chart of accounts and a pile of vendors with no explanation. Nobody wants to cancel something that might be important, so everything stays.

**Nonprofit pricing that expires.** Many software vendors offer discounted nonprofit plans, but the discount may require annual re-verification. Miss it, and the price jumps to the standard rate without a phone call.

**Free trials that convert.** A volunteer signs up for a fundraising tool for the spring gala. The trial rolls into an annual plan in June. The gala is long over.

**Personal cards and reimbursements.** Subscriptions paid by a board member or staff member and reimbursed later never show up as a recurring pattern in your bank feed. You only see them as scattered reimbursement requests.

**Overlapping tools.** Three people each picked a different tool for the same job. One chose a scheduling app, one a shared calendar add-on, one a project board. All three are still being paid for.

## How to run a subscription audit in one afternoon

You do not need special software. You need twelve months of bank and credit card statements, your general ledger, and someone with the authority to say yes or no. A short, practical sequence works well:

1. **Pull every recurring charge.** Sort twelve months of transactions by vendor name. Anything that appears monthly, quarterly or once a year at the same amount goes on the list.
2. **Add the hidden ones.** Check reimbursement requests, PayPal or other payment processors, and any card held by a staff member, board member or volunteer.
3. **Assign an owner to each line.** A name, not a department. If no one claims it, that is useful information.
4. **Record the renewal date and cancellation terms.** Annual plans often require 30 days' notice. A cancellation that comes one day late costs a full year.
5. **Mark each line keep, downgrade, consolidate or cancel.** Be honest about who actually used the tool in the last 90 days.
6. **Move the survivors into a single schedule** that lists vendor, monthly cost, annual cost, owner, renewal date and the program or grant it supports.

Most boards are surprised by two things. The total is bigger than they guessed, and a handful of lines have no owner at all.

## Grants, restrictions and the Form 990

This is where subscription creep stops being a budgeting annoyance and starts affecting compliance.

If a funder reimburses part of your software costs, the charge generally has to fall inside the grant period and relate to the funded work. A subscription that began under a grant and keeps running after the grant ends should not keep getting coded to that grant. A funder who requires an audit, or simply asks for backup, will notice.

Then there is the Form 990. Nonprofits report expenses by function: program services, management and general, and fundraising. A donor database is mostly fundraising. A scheduling tool for a youth program is program expense. Your accounting software subscription is management and general. When every software charge is coded to "office expense," your functional allocation gets blurry, and that blurriness shows up in the ratios your board and funders look at. If you want a reminder of how these filings work, our [nonprofit accounting page](/industries/nonprofits) covers Form 990 and 990-T preparation and board-ready financial statements.

A clean subscription schedule makes this easier. Each line already carries a function and, where it applies, a grant. Allocation becomes a matter of reading the list instead of reconstructing it in March.

## Set a few rules so it does not creep back

An audit helps for about a year. Rules keep the savings.

- **One approver for new recurring charges.** Anything that auto-renews, including free trials, needs a yes from the executive director or treasurer before a card goes in.
- **A shared organizational email for signups.** Renewal notices go to a mailbox that survives staff changes, such as billing@ at your domain, not a personal inbox.
- **One card for subscriptions.** Put recurring charges on a single organizational card with a modest limit, so the monthly statement is the review tool.
- **Calendar the renewal, not the cancellation.** Set a reminder 45 days before each annual renewal date and decide then.
- **A quarterly five-minute review.** The treasurer reads the subscription schedule at the board meeting. If it takes longer than five minutes, there are too many lines.

## Make the savings count

Cancelling a subscription only helps if the money goes somewhere on purpose. Before the audit, decide where the freed dollars will go. A few ideas that tend to land well with boards:

- Top up an operating reserve, even $150 a month.
- Cover the deductible gap on an insurance policy. ## Common Questions

**How often should a nonprofit review its subscriptions?**

A full audit once a year is enough for most organizations, with a quick quarterly check by the treasurer. Tie the annual review to budget season, so decisions about cancelling or downgrading flow straight into next year's numbers. Add a review any time a staff member or board officer leaves.

**Are software subscriptions an allowable grant expense?**

Often yes, but only when the grant terms allow it, the cost falls inside the grant period and it supports the funded work. A tool shared across programs usually needs a documented allocation. Check your award agreement, and keep the invoice and your allocation method in the grant file.

**Where do subscriptions go on a nonprofit's Form 990?**

They are reported as expenses in the functional categories: program services, management and general, or fundraising, depending on what the tool supports. A donor database leans toward fundraising, while accounting software sits in management and general. Shared tools may need a reasonable, consistent allocation.
