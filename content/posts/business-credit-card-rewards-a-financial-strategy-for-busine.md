Most Laramie non-profits already have a business credit card. Somebody on staff opened it for conference registrations or software subscriptions, and the rewards have been piling up ever since without anyone deciding what to do with them. A treasurer finds out at year-end that the organization is sitting on 80,000 points, a bookkeeper wonders where they belong in the books, and a board member asks whether this is a tax problem. It can be a small win or a small mess, depending on a few choices you make early.

This post walks through how to treat card rewards as a deliberate cash-flow tool for a non-profit: which card structure fits which spending, how the tax treatment generally works, and where grant rules and board oversight come in.

## Rewards belong in the board's conversation

A rewards card is a financing and cash-management decision. It is not a perk for whoever happens to hold the plastic.

Here is the basic math. Say your organization runs $10,000 a month through cards: program supplies, software, travel to a regional conference, event deposits. That is $120,000 a year. A flat 1.5% cash-back card returns $1,800. A card with a $95 annual fee and 3% back on your biggest category might return more, or it might return less if your spending is spread thin. Neither answer is obvious until someone looks at twelve months of statements.

The bigger lever is timing. A card with a monthly billing cycle and a grace period gives you roughly 25 to 55 days between a purchase and the cash leaving your bank account. For an organization waiting on a reimbursement from a funder, that float can smooth out a tight month. It only works if the balance is paid in full. Interest at typical card rates will erase a year of rewards in one or two billing cycles, so a non-profit that carries a balance should skip the rewards conversation and fix the cash flow first.

## Three Laramie non-profits, three different cards

The right card follows the spending pattern. These are illustrative examples, not specific clients, but the patterns are common.

| Organization type | Typical card spending | Card structure that tends to fit |
|---|---|---|
| Youth or education program | Supplies, field trips, software, snacks and event materials | Flat-rate cash back, no annual fee, many small purchases |
| Arts or community venue | Event deposits, marketing, vendor payments, ticketing fees | Higher rewards in one or two categories, if the volume justifies a fee |
| Association with a travel budget | Conference travel, lodging, member meetings | Travel card with a modest fee, if airfare and hotels exceed about $15,000 a year |

A few rules of thumb hold across all three.

**Count the fee against the rewards.** An annual fee of $95 needs about $6,300 a year in spending at 1.5% just to break even, before you count any bonus categories. Many non-profits are better off with a no-fee card.

**Be cautious with sign-up bonuses.** A bonus that requires $4,000 of spending in three months can push an organization to pull purchases forward, which defeats the point of managing cash.

**Prefer cash back over points for most organizations.** Points are harder to value, harder to record, and easier to lose track of when staff turn over. Cash back or statement credits create a clean, dated entry.

**Skip personal-style perks.** Airport lounge access and companion tickets benefit one person. A rewards program that mostly serves the cardholder invites questions the board does not want to field.

## Rewards are usually a price reduction, not income

Business owners ask whether card rewards are taxable. For purchases, the general view is that cash back or points earned from spending are treated as a rebate or discount on what you bought, not as income. Your cost goes down slightly, and nothing new shows up on a return.

A few situations are different, and they are worth knowing before you act:

- **Bonuses with no purchase requirement.** A deposit bonus or a payment for simply opening an account can be treated as income, and the issuer may send a Form 1099-MISC.
- **Co-branded or affinity cards.** If a card carries your organization's name and the issuer pays you for each swipe by members or donors, that payment is a different animal. Royalty-type income often falls outside unrelated business income, but the details matter. A Form 990-T question deserves a conversation before you sign anything.
- **Personal use.** If an employee earns points on organization-paid travel and keeps them, you have created a personal benefit question. A written policy fixes that.

For general background on federal small-entity guidance, the [IRS Small Business and Self-Employed Tax Center](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) is a good starting point. For anything specific to your exempt status, ask a CPA who handles non-profit returns every week.

## Check your grant terms before you redeem a point

This is where a non-profit differs most from a for-profit company. If a grant or contract pays for a purchase, the rewards from that purchase may not be yours to keep.

Many federal awards and some foundation agreements treat rebates, discounts and similar credits as reductions of the cost charged to the grant. If you bought $6,000 of program supplies on a card with 2% back, the funder may expect the cost to net out to $5,880. Interest and late fees are typically unallowable costs as well, so a card balance carried past the due date can leave you paying out of unrestricted funds.

The practical fix is simple. Before you route grant-funded spending to a rewards card, read the award terms and ask the program officer if the language is unclear. Some organizations keep grant-funded purchases on a separate no-rewards card or pay those vendors by check or ACH, and use the rewards card only for unrestricted spending. That keeps the audit trail clean when a funder asks for support.

## Write a card policy the board can approve

A two-page policy prevents most of the problems above. Boards that approve one tend to avoid surprises when a bookkeeper leaves or a new treasurer steps in, which are two of the most common moments when card practices fall apart.

A workable policy covers:

1. **Who may hold a card** and the monthly limit for each holder, for example $2,500 for a program director and $750 for a volunteer coordinator.
2. **What the card may buy,** and what it may not (personal items, cash advances, anything restricted by a grant).
3. **Who owns the rewards.** The organization does. Individual cardholders do not.
4. **Documentation.** An itemized receipt or a short written explanation for every charge, submitted within a set number of days.
5. **Payment.** The balance is paid in full each month from a designated account, approved by someone other than the cardholder.
6. **Redemption.** Rewards are redeemed on a set schedule, such as quarterly, and credited to a specific account.

That last point matters more than it seems. Rewards left to pile up for three years create a liability of attention, and they can expire or vanish if the program changes.

## Record rewards the same way every month

The tax treatment is rarely the hard part. Reconciliation is.

A tidy approach looks like this. Each month, reconcile the card statement to the books, coding every charge to the correct program, grant and expense category. When rewards post as a statement credit, record them as a reduction of the related expense, or in a designated account if your policy says so, and be consistent year to year. For restricted funds, make sure the credit lands in the same fund as the original purchase.

## Common Questions

**Are credit card rewards taxable for a non-profit?**

Rewards earned from purchases are generally treated as a price reduction, not income. Sign-up bonuses that require no spending, and payments from a co-branded card that uses your organization's name, can be treated differently. Check with your CPA before accepting either, since they may affect Form 990-T reporting.

**How do I choose a business credit card for a non-profit?**

Start with twelve months of actual card spending. If purchases are small and spread across many categories, a flat-rate cash-back card with no annual fee usually wins. A fee card only makes sense when one category, like conference travel, is large enough to cover the fee several times over.

**Can we keep rewards from grant-funded purchases?**

Not always. Many federal awards and some foundation agreements treat rebates and discounts as credits against the cost charged to the grant. Read the award terms first, and when in doubt, put grant-funded spending on a separate no-rewards card or pay by check or ACH.
