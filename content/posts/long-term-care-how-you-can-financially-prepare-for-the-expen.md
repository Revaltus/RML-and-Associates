Most people can name their monthly mortgage payment and their 403(b) balance. Very few can say what a private room in a nursing home costs per month, and fewer still know who would pay it. That gap is where long-term care plans fall apart. The bill usually arrives after a fall, a diagnosis, or a hospital discharge, and the decisions get made in a week instead of over ten years.

For people who work in the non-profit world, the gap is often wider. Executive directors, program staff and board members in Laramie tend to have mission-driven careers, solid retirement accounts built through 403(b) plans, and very little slack in the household budget. Care costs that would strain anyone can quickly threaten decades of careful saving.

This post treats long-term care as what it is: a financial risk that gets funded years ahead, through a mix of insurance, veterans benefits and savings.

## What long-term care costs, in round numbers

Prices vary by facility, and you should get local quotes before you plan around any figure. The numbers below are round figures for illustration, not quotes from any provider.

| Type of care | Illustrative monthly cost | Illustrative annual cost |
|---|---|---|
| In-home aide, 30 hours a week at $32 an hour | $4,160 | about $50,000 |
| Assisted living | $5,500 | $66,000 |
| Nursing home, private room | $10,000 | $120,000 |

Now put a time frame on it. A three-year stay in a nursing home at $10,000 a month is $360,000. Someone who spends two years at home with an aide and then moves to a facility for a year could easily land in the same range.

Medicare does not cover most of this. It pays for limited skilled nursing facility stays after a qualifying hospital stay, with copays starting on day 21. It does not pay for custodial care, which is the help with bathing, dressing, eating and moving around that makes up most long-term care. That is why the funding has to come from somewhere else.

## Three funding sources and where each one fits

Most workable plans combine more than one source. Here is how they compare.

| Source | Best fit | Main catch |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term care insurance (traditional or hybrid) | Healthy people in their 50s and early 60s who want to transfer some of the risk | Premiums rise with age, and traditional policies can see rate increases after purchase |
| VA Aid and Attendance | Wartime veterans and surviving spouses who need daily help and meet income and net worth limits | Strict eligibility rules and a look-back on recent asset transfers |
| Personal savings and investments | People with enough retirement assets to absorb a multi-year event | Market losses in the wrong year can shrink the fund when you need it |

No single row works for everyone. The right blend depends on age, health, assets and whether you or a spouse served in the military.

## Long-term care insurance vs self-funding

The honest answer is that this is a risk-tolerance question with a math problem inside it.

Insurance makes the most sense when a long care event would force you to sell assets or would cut into what a spouse needs to live on. You pay a known premium now to cap an unknown expense later. Hybrid policies, which pair life insurance or an annuity with a care benefit, cost more upfront but avoid the "use it or lose it" feeling of a traditional policy. Whichever type you consider, ask for the insurer's history of rate increases, the daily or monthly benefit, the benefit period and the inflation protection. A policy that pays $200 a day today may cover far less of the bill in 25 years.

Self-funding makes the most sense when your investable assets are large enough that a $360,000 event is painful but survivable. Picture a 58-year-old program director in Laramie with $450,000 in a 403(b) and a paid-off home. Setting aside a dedicated care reserve, say $300,000 earmarked and invested more conservatively as she nears 75, may beat paying premiums for 25 years. If her balance were $120,000, that same reserve is out of reach, and insurance or benefits would have to carry more of the load.

The middle path is common. People buy a smaller insurance benefit that covers the first few years or a share of daily costs, then self-fund the rest. That keeps premiums manageable and leaves savings intact for a longer event.

## Veterans aid and attendance benefit

This is the most overlooked source in the plan. The VA's [Aid and Attendance benefit](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/) is an added amount paid on top of the VA Pension for eligible wartime veterans and surviving spouses who need help with daily activities, are bedridden, live in a nursing home, or have significant vision loss. It is not available to everyone who served. Service dates, income and net worth all matter, and the VA sets the limits.

Two practical points belong in your planning:

- **Count assets carefully.** The VA applies a net worth limit, and it also looks back at asset transfers made in the prior 36 months. Giving money to children to qualify can backfire, so talk with an accredited VA claims agent or elder-law attorney before moving anything.
- **Apply with documentation ready.** Medical records, proof of service and a record of medical expenses all feed the application, and the process can take months.

For a veteran in Albany County, the benefit may not cover the full bill. It can still reduce how much the family has to draw from savings each month, and that compounds over a multi-year stay.

Medicaid is the fallback for people with very limited assets, and it carries a five-year look-back on gifts. The takeaway is the same as with the VA: the time to arrange your finances is years before care starts, not after.

## Tax treatment that helps the math

Long-term care is also a tax topic. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) explains how qualified long-term care services and premiums can count as medical expenses. Premiums for qualified policies are included up to an age-based annual limit. Unreimbursed qualified care for a chronically ill person can count too.

There is a catch. Medical expenses are deductible only if you itemize and only the portion above 7.5 percent of adjusted gross income. On a $75,000 AGI, the first $5,625 of expenses produces no deduction. A year with a $60,000 care bill clears that threshold easily, while ordinary years with modest premiums often do not.

Bunching helps. If you can time elective medical costs or pay certain care expenses in the same tax year, you may clear the threshold once instead of missing it every year. Our post on [planning for unexpected medical expenses at a Laramie non-profit](/resources/why-tax-advantaged-planning-is-the-real-defense-against-medi) covers HSAs, FSAs and the medical expense deduction in more detail. If you are on a high-deductible health plan, an HSA can also serve as a quiet long-term care fund, since qualified withdrawals are tax free.

## Why this matters for Laramie non-profit staff and boards

Non-profit careers come with a few specific pressure points.

**Retirement savings are real but modest.** A 403(b) built on non-profit salaries is a good foundation. It is rarely large enough to absorb a $360,000 event without a plan for it. Because withdrawals from pre-tax accounts are taxable income, a $120,000 year of care paid from a 403(b) can push you into a higher bracket than the cost alone suggests. The order and timing of withdrawals matter.

**Founders and longtime directors often do not retire on a schedule.** A founding executive director who stays 30 years may reach 70 without a funded care plan, because the organization always came first. Board members who notice this should treat it as a succession and compensation conversation, not a private one.

**Caregiving can pull a key person out of the job.** A development director or finance manager who leaves to care for a parent is a classic reason organizations call us on short notice. Planning for care on the personal side protects continuity on the organizational side. If your non-profit offers benefits, consider whether a voluntary group long-term care option or a financial-wellness session for staff fits your budget and funder rules.

**Rural logistics add cost.** If the nearest facility with an open bed is an hour away, family travel, lodging and time off work become part of the real price tag. Budget for them.

Boards that watch the organization's reserves closely should apply the same discipline to their own. Our guide to [keeping a balanced investment portfolio on a schedule](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) works for personal care reserves too: sort money into buckets by when you will need it, and rebalance on a calendar.

## A simple funding plan you can start this year

1. **Price it locally.** Call two or three Laramie-area providers and ask for current monthly rates for assisted living, memory care and in-home aides. Write the numbers down.
2. **Set a target.** Multiply the monthly cost you would likely need by 36. That is a reasonable planning figure for a multi-year event.
3. **Check for veterans benefits.** If you or a spouse served during a wartime period, find out now whether Aid and Attendance could apply, and keep service records in one place.
4. **Compare a policy quote with a savings plan.** Look at what premiums would cost through age 85 versus what you would need to set aside and invest to reach the same target.
5. **Review the tax picture.** Ask your CPA how premiums, HSA contributions and a future medical deduction fit with your return. Our [personal income tax](/personal-income-tax) page explains how we prepare returns and plan ahead.
6. **Revisit every two or three years.** Health, assets and insurance markets change.

A 15-year runway changes everything. At 55, you can build a reserve gradually, buy a policy while you are still insurable, and spread the cost. At 75, you may be left with only the options that remain.

## Common Questions

**When should I start planning for long-term care?**

The mid-50s to early 60s is the sweet spot. You are likely still healthy enough to qualify for insurance at a reasonable premium, and you have 15 to 20 years for savings to grow. Waiting until a health event limits your options makes every path more expensive.

**Does Medicare pay for nursing home care?**

Only in limited cases. Medicare covers skilled nursing facility care for a short period after a qualifying hospital stay, with copays starting on day 21. It does not cover custodial care, which is most of what long-term care involves, so you need another funding source.

**Can I deduct long-term care insurance premiums?**

Sometimes. Under IRS Publication 502, qualified premiums count as medical expenses up to an age-based limit, but only if you itemize and your total medical costs exceed 7.5 percent of adjusted gross income. Many people fall below that line in ordinary years.
