Most advice about leasing versus buying a car assumes you are hoping to write it off. A non-profit is not. If your Laramie organization is tax-exempt, Section 179, bonus depreciation and lease deductions don't lower your tax bill, because there is no tax bill to lower. That one fact flips the usual dealership pitch, and it means the decision comes down to cash flow, grant rules, reporting and how many miles your staff really drive.

Here is the framework we walk board treasurers and executive directors through when a van, a pickup or a staff sedan comes up for replacement.

## Why the usual tax argument flips for a tax-exempt organization

A for-profit owner who buys a work vehicle can often deduct a large share of the cost in year one. [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) lays out how Section 179 and depreciation work, including the caps on passenger vehicles. Lease payments, by contrast, are deducted over the lease term, with a small income inclusion for pricier vehicles. Those trade-offs drive a lot of the "buy it before December 31" advice.

None of it helps a 501(c)(3). Your organization doesn't pay federal income tax on income related to its mission, so a deduction has nothing to offset. When a salesperson says "you can write this off," the honest answer is that it doesn't matter to you.

What does matter:

- The total cash that leaves your account over the time you will actually use the vehicle
- Whether a grant or donor restriction allows the purchase
- How the vehicle appears on your balance sheet and your audit
- What happens when your needs change in year three

## When a tax deduction still matters

There are a few narrow cases where the tax side comes back into play.

If the vehicle is used in an unrelated trade or business, such as a thrift shop's delivery van hauling goods for a revenue stream that isn't tied to your mission, that activity can generate unrelated business income reported on Form 990-T. Depreciation or lease costs allocated to that activity can reduce it. You would need clean mileage logs showing how much use is mission-related and how much is not.

Personal use is the other one. If a director takes the organization's car home and uses it for errands, the value of that personal use is generally a taxable fringe benefit and belongs on their W-2. It is easy to miss, and it is one of the cleaner ways a well-meaning organization ends up with an uncomfortable conversation later.

If a donor gives you a vehicle, the rules change again, and we would want to see the paperwork before you sell or keep it.

## A Laramie food pantry van, lease versus buy

Here is an illustration with round numbers, not a quote. Say a Laramie food pantry needs a cargo van that runs routes to Rock River, Centennial and the east side of town. The van costs $42,000.

| | Lease (36 months) | Buy (60-month loan at 7%) |
|---|---|---|
| Cash at signing | $2,500 | $0 |
| Monthly payment | $549 | about $832 |
| Payments over 3 years | $19,764 | $29,952 |
| Loan balance after 36 months | None | about $18,600 |
| Estimated van value after 36 months | Returned to lender | about $27,000 |
| Net cash cost over 3 years | about $22,300 | about $21,500 |
| Mileage cap | 12,000 per year | None |
| Who owns it at the end | The lender | The pantry |

Over three years the two are nearly even, with buying ahead by under $1,000 in this example. The monthly payment is the number that looks cheaper on the lease, and it is the number that misleads people. The lease frees up about $283 a month. Buying leaves the pantry holding an asset worth roughly $8,400 more than the loan balance.

Now change one input. If the van will run 20,000 miles a year, a 12,000-mile lease adds overage charges, often 15 to 25 cents per mile. At 8,000 extra miles a year over three years, that is 24,000 miles, or somewhere between $3,600 and $6,000 in penalties. Buying wins clearly.

Change a different input. If the pantry is rolling out a program that might end when a grant expires in 24 months, a lease with an early termination clause may look worse on paper but be safer in practice. Owning a van you no longer need means selling it, and selling takes time.

## Driving habits in Wyoming that change the math

Miles are where Wyoming organizations get surprised. Laramie is 7,200 feet up, roughly 45 minutes from Cheyenne on I-80 and about 60 minutes from Fort Collins. A program coordinator who drives to a funder meeting in Denver, a site visit in Rawlins and a board dinner in Cheyenne can burn through 12,000 miles in under ten months without noticing.

A few patterns we see:

- **Under 10,000 miles a year, mostly in town.** Leasing can work well, especially if you want a newer vehicle with a warranty and predictable repair costs.
- **15,000 miles or more, or regular highway trips.** Buying usually costs less per mile, since you are not paying for excess mileage or returning a vehicle with wind, gravel and winter wear.
- **Unpredictable use.** If you can't say with confidence what next year looks like, price both options with a higher mileage estimate than you think you need.

Winter matters too. Hail, cracked windshields from gravel on US-287 and the general wear of Wyoming roads all show up at lease return as "excess wear and tear" charges. Read the wear definitions before you sign.

## Is owning a vehicle even the right answer?

Sometimes the cheapest vehicle is the one you don't buy. If staff or volunteers drive a few thousand miles a year for the organization, reimbursing mileage can cost far less than carrying a vehicle, insurance and maintenance. Employees can be reimbursed at the IRS standard mileage rate, which changes annually, so check the current number. Volunteers can be reimbursed tax-free only up to a much lower statutory charitable rate of 14 cents per mile, and anything above that is generally taxable to them. We covered how that plays out in our post on [tax deductions for volunteering](/resources/your-time-is-priceless-but-the-irs-wont-deduct-it-what-you-c).

If the number of miles is high and the use is predictable, a vehicle usually wins. If it is low and scattered, reimbursement usually does.

## Grants, boards and audits shape the answer

This is the part most auto blogs skip, and it is often the deciding factor.

**Grant restrictions.** Many funders limit or require prior approval for equipment purchases, and federal awards have their own thresholds and conditions on titles and disposal. A lease payment may be an allowable operating cost when a purchase is not, or the reverse. Check the award terms before you talk to a dealer, not after.

**Lease accounting.** Under current accounting standards (ASC 842), a non-profit with a lease longer than 12 months generally records a right-of-use asset and a matching liability on its balance sheet. So a lease is no longer "off the books" the way it once was. If your organization is audited or a lender reads your statements, a 36-month vehicle lease will show up.

**Board approval and capitalization policy.** Many organizations have a threshold, such as $5,000, above which purchases are capitalized and need board sign-off. A lease can slip under the radar in a way a purchase cannot, and then a treasurer finds it in the books. Settle who approves vehicle commitments before the paperwork lands.

**Insurance.** Coverage differs for owned, leased and hired vehicles, and lenders on leases often require higher limits. Our post on [sizing insurance coverage for a Laramie non-profit](/resources/insuring-where-it-counts-avoid-over-insuring-in-the-wrong-pl) explains how to match limits to your actual exposure.

## Financing and cash reserves

If you buy, the interest rate matters more than most people expect. The Federal Reserve's [G.19 consumer credit report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) publishes average new-car loan rates, which gives you a benchmark for judging the rate a dealer offers. A 1.5 percentage point difference on a $42,000, 60-month loan is roughly $1,700 in interest.

Then ask where the down payment comes from. Paying $8,000 in cash from operating reserves might leave you thin for a month when a grant reimbursement is late. If the organization holds board-designated reserves, our piece on [keeping a balanced investment portfolio on a schedule](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) explains how to decide which bucket a vehicle purchase should draw from.

Megan Hanneman, CGMA, works with management teams on cost analysis and budgeting. A vehicle decision is a good place for it: build a five-year cost model with fuel, insurance, maintenance and the replacement cycle, then compare both options on the same basis.

## Questions to settle before you sign

1. How many miles will this vehicle drive each year, with a realistic buffer?
2. Does any grant or restricted gift cover or limit this purchase?
3. Who approves the commitment, and does it fit your capitalization policy?
4. Will any use be unrelated to your mission, or personal?
5. What is the early termination cost if the program ends?
6. Is reimbursing mileage cheaper than owning?

If you can answer those six, the lease versus buy choice usually becomes obvious. If you can't, that is the useful conversation to have before anyone talks monthly payments.

## Common Questions

**Can a non-profit take the Section 179 vehicle deduction?**

Generally no, because a tax-exempt organization has no income tax to reduce on mission-related activity. The deduction can matter only if the vehicle supports an unrelated business activity reported on Form 990-T. Even then, only the business-use share counts, so keep a mileage log.

**Is leasing cheaper than buying for a non-profit?**

Leasing has the lower monthly payment, but over three to five years buying often costs about the same or less, since you keep the vehicle's value. Leasing tends to win with low, predictable mileage and a short program horizon. Buying wins for high mileage or long-term use.

**Does a vehicle lease show up on a non-profit's financial statements?**

Yes, in most cases. Under ASC 842, a lease longer than 12 months is generally recorded as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability. Auditors, lenders and funders reading your statements will see it, so include it in your budget and board reporting.
