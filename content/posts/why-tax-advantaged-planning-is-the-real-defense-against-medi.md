A nonprofit executive director in Laramie can do everything right. Family health coverage, six months of operating reserves at work, a personal savings account with a respectable balance. Then a surgery, a few weeks of lost work, and a string of specialist visits in Fort Collins or Denver add up to a $12,000 hospital bill, and the savings cushion starts draining fast. The cushion did its job. It just wasn't built to do the whole job.

This post is about the other half of the defense: the tax-advantaged accounts and deductions that make each dollar you spend on medical care cost less. It is written for the people who run and work at nonprofits, because your staff, your board treasurer and you as a leader often face this exact squeeze: modest salaries, high-deductible coverage, and a benefits package the organization has some control over.

## Why a savings cushion is only half the plan

An emergency fund is the right first move. It is also easy to overestimate. The Federal Reserve's [Report on the Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/report-economic-well-being-us-households.htm) has consistently found that roughly a third of adults could not cover a $400 emergency expense using cash or its equivalent. A real medical event is many times that, and a high-deductible plan can put the first several thousand dollars squarely on you.

There is a second problem. Savings are after-tax money. To put $4,000 in a savings account, you first earned about $5,700 in gross pay, because federal income tax and Social Security and Medicare tax came out first. Every dollar you spend from that account on a medical bill has already been taxed once.

Tax-advantaged tools change that math. They do not replace an emergency fund, but they make the fund last longer and, in the case of an HSA, keep working after the crisis is over. That is how you avoid medical debt: not by hoping the bill never comes, but by arranging your finances so that when it does, you are paying with discounted dollars.

## How an HSA stretches the same dollar further

A Health Savings Account is the strongest tool of the three, and it has a triple advantage. Contributions are deductible (or pulled from your paycheck before income tax), growth inside the account is untaxed, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. Nothing else in the tax code does all three.

To contribute, you must be covered by a high-deductible health plan and have no disqualifying coverage. For 2025, the HSA limit is $4,300 for self-only coverage and $8,550 for family coverage, plus an extra $1,000 if you are 55 or older. The IRS adjusts these numbers each year, so confirm the current figures before you set contributions.

Here is what that looks like on a $4,000 medical bill for someone in the 22% federal bracket whose HSA contributions run through the employer's paycheck deduction plan:

| | Paid from checking account | Paid from HSA (paycheck deduction) |
|---|---|---|
| Gross pay needed | about $5,690 | $4,000 |
| Federal income tax (22%) | about $1,250 | $0 |
| Social Security and Medicare (7.65%) | about $435 | $0 |
| Left to pay the bill | $4,000 | $4,000 |

That is roughly $1,690 of pay you did not have to earn to cover the same bill. These are illustrative figures, not a quote for your return, but the pattern holds for most taxpayers. If you fund an HSA on your own rather than through the employer, you still get the income tax deduction. You just miss the Social Security and Medicare savings.

Three details trip people up:

- **The account has to exist first.** Expenses incurred before the HSA is open generally are not qualified, so open it before the surgery is scheduled, not after.
- **The money rolls over.** Unlike a spending account that resets, an unused HSA balance stays yours, even if you change jobs.
- **Records matter.** You can reimburse yourself years later for a qualified expense, but only if you can prove the date and amount. A simple folder of bills and explanation-of-benefits statements is enough.

Wyoming has no state income tax, so for Laramie residents the savings are federal. If you live or work in Colorado or Nebraska, your state return adds another layer we check separately.

## What your nonprofit can do as the employer

Your organization pays no income tax, but your employees do, and the benefits plan is where the nonprofit can help them most. Three decisions sit with the board or the executive director.

**Set up a cafeteria plan.** This is the plan document that lets employees fund HSAs and flexible spending accounts with pre-tax paycheck deductions. Without one, the Social Security and Medicare savings in the table above disappear. The paperwork is modest, and it costs the organization little.

**Consider an employer HSA contribution.** Even $500 a year per eligible employee is tax-free to the employee and is often cheaper for the organization than a raise of the same size, because it avoids employer-side Social Security and Medicare tax on the contribution when made through a cafeteria plan. For a Laramie nonprofit with eight employees, that is a $4,000 line in the budget, and it is a concrete answer when someone asks what the organization does for health costs.

**Put it in the budget before the crisis.** Benefit costs belong in your forecast. Megan Hanneman, CGMA, works with management teams on budgeting and forecasting, and benefit contributions are one of the line items that are easy to model now and painful to improvise later. If a funder requires an audit or a bookkeeper leaves mid-year, a documented benefits plan is one less thing to untangle. Our [nonprofit accounting work](/industries/nonprofits) already covers Form 990 and 990-T filing, board-ready financials and audit preparation, so benefits fit naturally into the same conversation.

One caution: HSA contributions through a cafeteria plan must follow nondiscrimination rules, so they cannot tilt toward highly paid staff. Confirm the design with your CPA before you announce anything.

## Using the medical expense deduction when the bill is large

The itemized medical expense deduction is the safety net for bills the HSA did not cover. It is also the most misunderstood. You can deduct only the portion of unreimbursed medical costs above 7.5% of your adjusted gross income, and only if your total itemized deductions beat the standard deduction.

A worked example: say your AGI is $80,000. The 7.5% floor is $6,000. If you have $18,000 in unreimbursed medical costs that year, $12,000 is deductible. At a 22% bracket, that is about $2,640 back. With $5,000 in costs, you deduct nothing.

That floor is why timing matters. If you know a procedure is coming, you can sometimes bunch elective care, such as dental work, vision, or a planned surgery, into the same calendar year as a bill you cannot avoid, so the total clears the floor. Deferring it to January resets the clock.

The list of what counts is longer than most people think. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) covers it, and it includes:

- Insurance premiums you pay with after-tax dollars (not those paid through an employer's pre-tax plan)
- Prescription drugs and insulin
- Mileage to medical appointments at a per-mile rate the IRS sets each year, which adds up when your specialist is 65 miles away in Fort Collins or about 130 miles away in Denver
- Lodging for out-of-town treatment, within limits
- Dental, vision and hearing care

Here is the catch with HSAs: you cannot deduct an expense you paid from the HSA, because it was already tax-free. Keep the two lanes separate on your return.

## Flexible spending accounts reward people who check the deadline

A health flexible spending account also uses pre-tax paycheck dollars. For 2025, the limit is $3,300, and unlike an HSA you do not need a high-deductible plan. The tradeoff is the use-it-or-lose-it rule. Employers may allow a carryover of up to $660 (for 2025) or a grace period of up to two and a half months, but not both, and some offer neither.

Two practical points for nonprofits. First, you generally cannot contribute to a standard health FSA and an HSA in the same year, though a limited-purpose FSA for dental and vision is allowed alongside an HSA. Second, if your organization offers an FSA, put the deadline on the staff calendar in October. Employees forfeit more money at year-end than they realize, and a reminder email costs nothing.

## A simple order of operations

If you are deciding where the next dollar goes, this order works for most households we talk with:

1. Build a starter cushion of one month of expenses so a bill doesn't land on a credit card.
2. If you have an HSA-eligible plan, contribute enough to cover your annual deductible, ideally through paycheck deductions.
3. Add the rest of your emergency fund, targeting three to six months of expenses.
4. Fill the HSA toward its annual limit if cash flow allows.
5. Keep medical records organized so the itemized deduction, if you qualify, takes minutes rather than days.

Organizations can mirror this: [match your insurance coverage to your net assets and cash flow](/resources/insuring-where-it-counts-avoid-over-insuring-in-the-wrong-pl), then put the benefits design in writing.

## Common Questions

**How much should I save for unexpected medical expenses?**

At minimum, save your plan's out-of-pocket maximum, which for 2025 can be as high as $8,300 for self-only and $16,600 for family coverage on an HSA-eligible plan. An HSA lets you build that amount with pre-tax dollars, so it takes less gross pay than a regular savings account.

**Can a nonprofit offer HSAs if it doesn't pay income tax?**

Yes. The organization offers the plan, and the tax benefit goes to employees. A cafeteria plan lets staff fund HSAs through paycheck deductions, and the nonprofit can add its own contribution. Contributions must follow nondiscrimination rules, so confirm the design with a CPA first.

**Is the medical expense deduction worth itemizing for?**

Only when your unreimbursed medical costs exceed 7.5% of AGI and your total itemized deductions beat the standard deduction. With an $80,000 AGI, the first $6,000 is not deductible. Bunching elective care into one year is the most common way to clear the floor.

## Plan before the bill arrives

The best time to set up an HSA, a cafeteria plan or a bunching strategy is before anyone is in a hospital bed.
