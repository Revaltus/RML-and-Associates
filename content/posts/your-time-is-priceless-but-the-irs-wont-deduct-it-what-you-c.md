Every January, someone in Laramie sits down with their tax software, remembers the 200 hours they gave to a local food pantry, animal shelter or youth program, and wonders where that goes on the return. The answer is nowhere. The IRS does not let you deduct the value of your time or your skills, no matter how many hours you give or what you would normally bill for them. The good news is that the money you spend while doing good often is deductible, and most volunteers never claim it.

This post covers what counts, what does not, and the records that keep a deduction safe if anyone ever asks. It is written for volunteers and board members of Laramie non-profit organizations, and for the staff who field their questions.

## Why your volunteer hours are not deductible

The rule is simple. You can deduct what you give up in dollars, not what you give up in time. If you are a CPA, a carpenter or a restaurant owner who donates 40 hours of work to a fundraiser, the tax code treats that labor as having no deductible value. [IRS Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526) says so plainly: you cannot deduct the value of your services.

That feels unfair, but it is also consistent. Your volunteer hours were never income, so there is nothing to offset. Your unreimbursed costs, on the other hand, are real money out of your pocket, and the IRS treats them as a charitable contribution.

## Out-of-pocket costs that do count

To be deductible, a cost has to be directly connected to your volunteer work, unreimbursed, and incurred for a qualified organization. For most non-profit organizations in Wyoming, that means a 501(c)(3) public charity. Here is what typically qualifies:

- **Supplies you buy for the organization.** Craft materials for a youth program, paint for a community building project, or printer paper and postage for a mailing.
- **Mileage in your own vehicle.** Driving to a food bank shift, hauling donations, or traveling to a board meeting.
- **Parking and tolls** paid while volunteering.
- **Uniforms the organization requires,** as long as they are not suitable for everyday wear. A branded event shirt you could wear to the grocery store does not qualify. A required costume or protective gear generally does.
- **Travel away from home overnight.** If you attend a statewide non-profit conference in Cheyenne or a regional training in Denver on the organization's behalf, your lodging, transportation and meals can qualify, provided there is no significant element of personal pleasure, recreation or vacation in the trip.
- **Cooking or event costs.** Ingredients you buy to prepare meals for a shelter, or supplies for a fundraiser, count. The cost of the food is deductible. Your cooking time is not.

## Costs that look deductible but are not

Some expenses feel connected to volunteering but fail the test. This table covers the ones that come up most often.

| Expense | Deductible? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Your hours or professional skills | No | The value of services is never deductible |
| Mileage to a board meeting | Yes | Unreimbursed travel for a qualified organization |
| Childcare while you volunteer | No | Personal expense, even though it enables the work |
| Everyday meals near home during a shift | No | Meals count only when you are away from home overnight |
| Event shirt you would wear anyway | No | Not a required uniform unsuitable for everyday use |
| Expenses the organization reimbursed | No | You were not out of pocket |
| Supplies bought for a school-fundraising booth | Yes | Direct, unreimbursed cost for a qualified organization |
| Gifts to a specific person in need | No | The recipient must be a qualified organization |
| Dues or gifts to a chamber of commerce or civic league | Generally no | Many are not 501(c)(3) charities |

Two of these catch people off guard. First, if the organization reimburses you, even partly, you can only deduct the unreimbursed part. Second, if you buy a ticket to a fundraising dinner, you can only deduct the amount above the fair market value of what you received. A $100 plate that includes a $40 meal is a $60 deduction.

## Mileage: a small rate that adds up

The charitable mileage rate is 14 cents per mile. Congress sets it by statute, so it does not move with gas prices the way the business mileage rate does. That surprises people who are used to the higher business rate.

You have two options. You can use the 14 cents per mile, or you can deduct your actual costs for gas and oil. Either way, you may add parking and tolls. You cannot deduct general vehicle costs like insurance, repairs or depreciation.

Here is a realistic example. A board treasurer in Laramie drives to Cheyenne for a statewide meeting, about 50 miles each way. That is 100 miles, or $14, plus perhaps $10 in parking. Do that eight times in a year and it comes to roughly $190. Add 1,500 miles of local volunteer driving for a food pantry, which is $210, and $90 in supplies. The total is about $490 in deductions.

That is not a life-changing number. It is still money you spent, and it is worth claiming correctly. Keep a simple log with the date, destination, purpose and miles for each trip. A note in your phone works fine.

## You have to itemize, and the organization has to qualify

This is where most volunteer deductions quietly disappear. Charitable contributions, including volunteer expenses, are claimed on Schedule A. That only helps if your total itemized deductions beat the standard deduction. For 2025, the standard deduction is $15,750 for single filers and $31,500 for married couples filing jointly.

A $490 volunteer deduction on its own will not clear that bar. But it stacks with everything else. If you pay mortgage interest, state and local taxes, and give cash gifts to your church or the university foundation, the volunteer costs can be the piece that makes itemizing worthwhile. If you are close to the line, it is worth running both versions of your return before you decide.

The qualified-organization test matters just as much. Before you claim anything, confirm the group is eligible to receive deductible contributions. The IRS has a search tool linked from its [Charities and Nonprofits hub](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits) that shows an organization's status. Many Laramie groups you volunteer with are churches, schools or 501(c)(3) charities. Others, like some civic leagues, booster clubs and advocacy groups, are not, and expenses for those are not deductible.

## Records that hold up if anyone asks

The rules get stricter as the dollars go up. Under $250 in total unreimbursed expenses for one organization, you need your own records: receipts, bank or card statements, and your mileage log. At $250 or more, you also need a written statement from the organization. Per Publication 526, that statement should include:

1. A description of the services you provided.
2. The date or dates you provided them.
3. Whether the organization gave you any goods or services to cover your expenses.

You need to have this acknowledgment in hand by the time you file, or by the filing deadline including extensions, whichever comes first. Ask for it in January, not April. Organizations get buried in requests at that time of year, and a volunteer who waits is often the one who ends up without the letter.

## What Laramie non-profit boards and staff can do

If you run or serve on a non-profit board, you can make this easier for your volunteers in three ways.

**Make the acknowledgment letter routine.** A one-page template that lists the volunteer's name, services, dates and a line stating whether anything was given in return takes about ten minutes to set up. It saves your treasurer a long thread of one-off requests every winter.

**Be clear about reimbursement.** If your policy is to reimburse mileage and supplies, say so in writing. Volunteers who are reimbursed cannot also deduct those costs, and confusion here leads to bad returns.

**Keep your own filings clean.** A well-organized acknowledgment process supports the giving data that ends up in your Form 990. Mistakes or late filings on the 990 and 990-T can put tax-exempt status at risk, so it pays to have someone who knows the forms review your donor records before they are filed. ## Common Questions

**Can I deduct the value of my volunteer hours?**

No. The IRS does not allow a deduction for the value of your time or services, even if you are a professional who would normally bill for them. Only your unreimbursed out-of-pocket costs, like supplies, mileage at 14 cents per mile, and overnight travel, can be deducted, and only if you itemize.

**Do I need a receipt for every volunteer expense?**

Keep a record of each one: a receipt, card statement or mileage log entry. If your total unreimbursed expenses for one organization reach $250, you also need a written acknowledgment from the organization describing your services and stating whether you received anything in return. Get it before you file.

**Can board members deduct travel to meetings?**

Yes, if the organization is a qualified charity and does not reimburse you. Mileage to board meetings, parking, and overnight travel for the organization's business can qualify. Keep a log with the date, destination, purpose and miles, and be careful to separate any personal portion of a trip.
