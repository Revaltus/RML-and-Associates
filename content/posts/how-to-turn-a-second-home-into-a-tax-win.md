Most second home owners in Laramie pick the place for the view, then find out later that the IRS cares a great deal about how many nights they actually slept there. A cabin in the Snowy Range, a condo in Fort Collins or a lake place in Colorado can be a quiet tax shelter or a rental business with its own paperwork. The difference comes down to a day count, and you control it.

This post walks through the second home tax rules that matter most, with a worked example in dollars. It also covers a few wrinkles that come up for people who work and volunteer in Laramie's nonprofit community: board members, executive directors, development staff and the organizations they serve.

## Three buckets decide how the IRS treats your second home

The IRS sorts every second home into one of three buckets based on two numbers: days you rent it at a fair price, and days you use it personally. The details are in [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527), and the short version looks like this.

| Your year | How the IRS treats it | What it means on your return |
|---|---|---|
| Rented fewer than 15 days | Personal residence | Rent is tax-free and not reported. Rental expenses are not deductible. |
| Rented 15 or more days, and personal use is more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days | Residence with rental use | Rent is reported. Expenses are deductible only up to the rental income, so no loss. |
| Rented 15 or more days, and personal use is no more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days | Rental property | Full rental deductions, including depreciation. A loss is possible, subject to passive loss limits. |

That first row is the part people like. If you rent the place for 14 days or fewer, the income is tax-free. A Laramie house rented for a handful of days around a Wyoming Cowboys home game or commencement weekend can bring in real money that never shows up on your return. This is the IRS 14-day rule for a second home, and it is one of the few places the tax code simply hands you a break.

The second and third rows are where owners get surprised. Crossing from one to the other can change your return by thousands of dollars.

## Which days count as personal use

Personal use is broader than "the nights I slept there." Under Publication 527, a day counts as personal use when:

- You or a family member use the home, even if the relative pays rent at a below-market rate.
- Someone uses it under a swap or reciprocal arrangement, like trading weeks with a friend's property.
- Anyone uses it while paying less than a fair rental price.

Days you spend there mostly doing repairs and maintenance generally do not count as personal use. A weekend of scraping and restaining the deck is a work weekend, as long as you actually work most of the day. Keep a log that says so.

Because fair rental price is the test, your brother paying you half of market rate for a week turns that week into a personal week. Rental days stay rental days only when the rent is real.

## A Snowy Range cabin, worked in dollars

Here is a realistic example. A Laramie couple owns a cabin in the Snowy Range. They rent it for 60 nights at an average of $200, so rental income is $12,000. Their yearly costs look like this:

- Mortgage interest: $8,000
- Property taxes: $2,400
- Utilities, insurance, cleaning and repairs: $6,000
- Depreciation, if the home qualifies as rental property: $9,000

**Scenario A: 14 personal days.** The couple stays 14 nights. That is not more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of 60 rental days (6 days), so the cabin is rental property. Total use is 74 days, so the rental share is 60 divided by 74, or about 81%. Deductible rental expenses come to roughly $20,600 against $12,000 of income. That is a paper loss of about $8,600.

Whether the loss helps now depends on the passive loss rules. If you actively participate in the rental and your modified adjusted gross income is $100,000 or less, you can generally deduct up to $25,000 of rental loss against other income. That allowance shrinks by 50 cents for every dollar of income over $100,000 and disappears at $150,000. A couple earning $120,000 could use the full $8,600 now. A couple at $160,000 would carry it forward to future years.

**Scenario B: 20 personal days.** Six more nights at the cabin pushes personal use past 14 days, so the cabin becomes a residence with rental use. Total use is 80 days and the rental share drops to 75%. Their share of interest and property taxes is $7,800, and operating costs are limited to what the $12,000 of income can absorb, about $4,200. Depreciation is zero. Net rental income is zero, and there is no loss.

Six extra nights cost this couple an $8,600 deduction. Nothing about the cabin changed. That is why we like to talk before the year starts rather than in March.

## Mortgage interest and property taxes still work in your favor

Even when the home is classed as a residence, you generally keep your mortgage interest and property tax deductions if you itemize. Interest on a second home is generally deductible on loans up to $750,000 in total acquisition debt across your main and second homes for loans taken out after December 15, 2017. The federal cap on state and local tax deductions still applies to property taxes.

On a rented vacation home, the share of interest and taxes that is not allocated to rental use moves to your personal itemized deductions. Nothing is wasted, but the deduction lands on a different part of the return, and whether it helps depends on whether you itemize at all. Many Laramie households take the standard deduction, so an itemized interest deduction may be worth less than it sounds.

## Nonprofit wrinkles that most second home articles skip

People in Laramie's nonprofit world use their personal property for the cause more often than they realize. A few situations are worth a second look.

**Donating a week at your cabin to a gala auction.** This is generous and popular. But Publication 527 treats it as personal use if the charity sells the stay and someone uses it. You also generally cannot deduct the value of donated use of your property. One donated week can push you over the 14-day line in the table above, so count it.

**Hosting a board retreat or staff planning weekend.** If the organization pays a fair rental price, those are rental days, and you report the income. If it pays little or nothing, those are personal days for you. Either way, write down the dates and the rate. A rental at an inflated price is also a problem, since the IRS and the organization's own conflict-of-interest policy will both want to see how the price was set.

**Receiving a donated vacation home.** If your organization is given a cabin or condo and then rents it out, rent from real property is generally excluded from unrelated business income. Debt on the property can change that. When there is a mortgage, the debt-financed rules may require a Form 990-T. We check this early, because a gift that creates an unexpected filing is easier to fix before the first rental season. Our [nonprofit accounting work](/industries/nonprofits) includes Form 990 and 990-T preparation, so these questions are routine for us.

**Buying with a salary that fluctuates.** Nonprofit compensation can be steady, but grant cycles, bonuses and spouse income can push you above or below the $100,000 to $150,000 passive loss phase-out range from year to year. Where you land in that range affects how useful a rental loss is, so the same cabin can be worth more in one year than in the next.

## Decide before you buy, not after the first rental

The question to answer is how you intend to use the place. Three plans, three different results:

- **Mostly personal, with a few rented days.** Keep rentals to 14 days or fewer and the income is tax-free. You give up rental deductions, and that is fine, since there is no income to offset.
- **Mixed use.** Rent it often enough to cover costs, and use it more than 14 days. Expect the income to be roughly offset by expenses, with no loss to claim.
- **Rental first.** Rent it most of the year and keep personal use at or under 14 days (or 10% of rental days, if that is higher). You get depreciation and potential losses, and also bookkeeping duties, sales and lodging tax filings, and a passive loss calculation each year.

A second home bought as an investment property follows the same rules as any other rental, and that is where [a CPA who works with real estate owners](/industries/real-estate) earns their fee. We can model the tax impact of each plan before you sign, using your actual income, loan terms and rental estimates. That includes the break-even number of rental nights.

## Records that make the numbers hold up

A calendar is the most useful document you can keep. Mark every night as rental, personal or maintenance, and note the rate for anything that is not a standard booking. Keep your closing statement, loan statements, property tax bills, cleaning invoices and any platform payout reports. If you rent for more than a few days, a separate bank account for the property keeps your personal and rental money apart.

The IRS also publishes a plain-language summary in [Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415) if you want a second source. Your [personal return](/personal-income-tax) is where all of this lands, so the calendar matters more than any software.

## Common Questions

**What is the IRS 14-day rule for a second home?**

If you rent your home for fewer than 15 days in a year, the rental income is tax-free and you do not report it. You also cannot deduct rental expenses. A separate 14-day test compares your personal use to rental days to decide whether the home counts as a residence or as rental property.

**Can I deduct a loss on a second home I rent out?**

Only if the home counts as rental property, meaning personal use is no more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days. Even then, passive loss rules apply. With active participation and income under $100,000, up to $25,000 may be deductible, phasing out completely at $150,000.

**Does a nonprofit board retreat at my cabin count as personal use?**

It does if the organization pays you less than a fair rental price, or nothing. If it pays the market rate, it counts as a rental day. Either way, document the dates and the rate, and check your nonprofit's conflict-of-interest policy before accepting payment.
