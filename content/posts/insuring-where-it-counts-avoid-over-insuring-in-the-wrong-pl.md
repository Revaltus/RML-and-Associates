Most nonprofit boards in Laramie handle insurance the same way. The agent sends a renewal quote, the treasurer compares the premium to last year's, and someone asks whether it can come down. That is a fair question, but it skips the one that matters: how much of a loss could your organization absorb without cutting a program?

The answer to "how much insurance coverage do you need" is not a list of policies. It is a calculation that starts with your net assets and your cash flow, and works outward from there.

## Start with what a loss would cost, not what a policy covers

A checklist asks, "Do we have general liability? Do we have D&O?" A risk-based review asks different questions:

- What could realistically go wrong this year?
- How likely is it, and how bad would it be?
- Could our reserves absorb it, or would we have to stop doing what donors fund us to do?

Insurance is a tool for losses you cannot afford to keep. Everything you can afford to keep, you can often self-insure. The goal is to spend premium dollars on the first group and stop paying for the second.

That is also why two nonprofits of the same size can need very different coverage. A food pantry running out of a leased building has a different exposure than a foundation with a small office and a large investment account.

## Know your cushion before you pick a deductible

Here is a hypothetical to make the math concrete. Picture a Laramie youth-services nonprofit with $800,000 in annual operating expenses and $200,000 in unrestricted cash reserves. That is three months of operations, which is a healthy position for an organization this size.

Donor-restricted money does not count toward the cushion. Restricted funds generally have to be spent on the purpose the donor named, so they are not available to pay for a damaged vehicle or a settled claim.

A useful starting point is to set the amount you will retain on any single loss at roughly 1 to 2 percent of annual operating expenses. For this organization, that is $8,000 to $16,000. It is a rule of thumb, not a formula to apply blindly, but it gives the board a number to react to instead of a vague sense of caution.

Once you have that number, deductibles stop being an afterthought. A $500 deductible on a property policy costs real premium dollars each year. If the organization could comfortably absorb $10,000, the board may be paying a premium for a benefit it does not need.

## Where nonprofits tend to over-insure

Over-insuring rarely looks like buying too many policies. It usually looks like paying too much for coverage on small, survivable losses.

**Low deductibles on small losses.** Frequent small claims are cheap to absorb and expensive to insure. Raising a deductible from $1,000 to $5,000 can reduce premium noticeably, and the organization takes on a loss it can easily afford.

**Limits set by habit.** Property limits should track replacement cost of what you actually own. If the office furniture and equipment would cost $40,000 to replace and the policy lists $150,000, someone is paying for $110,000 of coverage that will never be used.

**Overlapping policies.** A package policy, a separate event policy, and a rider the agent added three years ago can cover the same exposure twice. Ask for a one-page map showing which policy responds to which risk.

**Coverage on assets you could replace from cash.** A $3,000 laptop does not need its own scheduled coverage if the cash is already sitting in reserves.

None of this means running thin. It means putting each dollar of premium where a loss would actually hurt.

## Where the gaps usually hide

The flip side of over-insuring in the wrong places is under-insuring in the places that can end an organization. For nonprofits, a few exposures tend to get less attention than they deserve.

**Employee theft and fraud.** Crime or fidelity coverage is often missing or set at a token limit. The risk climbs when one person handles deposits, reconciliations and reporting, or when a bookkeeper or finance staff member leaves and the replacement inherits unclear controls. Coverage helps, but separation of duties helps more.

**Directors and officers liability.** Board members are volunteers, and many will not serve without D&O protection. Employment practices claims, funder disputes and regulatory questions can all land on the board's doorstep, even when no one did anything wrong.

**Donor and client data.** If you keep donor records, event registrations or client intake forms, a breach is a real cost. Notification, credit monitoring and legal fees add up quickly, and a general liability policy usually will not respond.

**Events and volunteers.** A fundraiser with 300 attendees, a 5K or a rodeo booth creates exposure that a basic policy may exclude or limit. Volunteer accident coverage is often cheap relative to the risk.

**Workers' compensation.** Rules are set at the state level, so ask your agent exactly what Wyoming requires for your employees and how volunteers are treated.

## A simple way to rank your exposures

Before the next renewal, have the finance committee fill in a table like this. The details will differ for your organization, but the pattern holds.

| Exposure | Likelihood | Severity | Usual approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Damaged equipment under $5,000 | Moderate | Low | Retain, with a higher deductible |
| Fire or water damage at a leased building | Low | High | Insure at replacement cost |
| Employee theft or fraud | Low | High | Insure, and tighten internal controls |
| Lawsuit naming board members | Low | High | Insure with D&O |
| Donor data breach | Moderate | Moderate to high | Insure, and review security |
| Injury at a fundraising event | Moderate | High | Insure, with event-specific limits |

The rows marked "retain" are where premium savings come from. The rows marked "insure" are where the money should go. If a high-severity row has no policy behind it, that is the first item on the board's agenda.

## What funders, landlords and the IRS expect

Sometimes the limit is not a choice. Landlords, grant agreements and government contracts often specify minimum liability limits, and some require the funder to be named as an additional insured. Check these documents before cutting any limits, because dropping below a contract minimum can put a grant at risk.

The tax side is narrower for nonprofits, but it is still worth knowing. Premiums are ordinary operating expenses on your Form 990. If the organization runs an unrelated business activity that is reported on Form 990-T, a reasonable share of insurance cost may be deductible against that income. [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535) covers how business insurance premiums are treated, and we can help you allocate costs sensibly between exempt and unrelated activities.

An insurance review also makes a stronger story for funders. When a board can show that its coverage was sized to its reserves and its contracts, it signals the kind of financial oversight funders look for.

## Bring the numbers to your agent

CPAs do not sell insurance, and that is useful. Your agent places coverage and knows the market. Your CPA knows your balance sheet, your reserves, your restricted funds and your cash flow, and has no commission riding on the answer.

Megan Hanneman, CGMA, works with management teams on budgeting, forecasting and cost analysis, which are the same tools a risk review needs. She can help your team model what a retained loss would do to your monthly cash position, so your conversation with the agent starts from your numbers, not the quote.

A practical sequence looks like this:

1. Pull your latest balance sheet and separate restricted from unrestricted net assets.
2. Calculate how many months of expenses your unrestricted cash covers.
3. Set a retention level the board is comfortable with.
4. List your top six exposures and rank them by severity.
5. Ask your agent to quote higher deductibles and to show every policy and limit on a single page.
6. Compare the premium savings to the added retained risk, and decide as a board.

## Common Questions

**How much insurance coverage does a nonprofit need?**

Enough to cover losses that would force you to cut programs or deplete reserves, plus any limits your contracts require. For most nonprofits that means liability, property, D&O and crime coverage at minimum. Retain smaller losses that unrestricted cash can absorb, and insure the ones it cannot.

**Should a nonprofit carry directors and officers insurance?**

In most cases, yes. Board members are volunteers who face real exposure from employment disputes, funder conflicts and regulatory questions. Many qualified candidates will not join a board without D&O coverage, so the premium often pays for itself in recruiting alone.

**How often should we review our coverage?**

At every renewal, and any time something material changes: a new lease, a major grant, a new program, a vehicle purchase or turnover in your finance staff. Compare limits to your current net assets and contract requirements, not last year's policy.
