The executive director of a Laramie nonprofit rarely misses a board meeting. What gets missed is lunch, a weekend, and, somewhere around April, the extension request for the Form 990. Nobody planned to skip it. The person who runs the programs, writes the grants, answers the funder emails and approves the invoices simply ran out of attention.

That is the financial case for self-care for business owners, and it applies just as much to people who run non-profit organizations. A nonprofit leader is an owner in every way that matters to the books: you make the spending calls, you sign off on the numbers, and you answer for the mistakes. When your judgment wears thin, the organization pays for it.

This post is not medical advice. We are CPAs, not therapists. What we can speak to is what tired decision-making does to a set of financial statements, because we see it up close.

## Why tired leaders make expensive mistakes

Burnout rarely arrives as a dramatic collapse. It shows up as small deferrals. The bank reconciliation waits another week. The grant report goes in on deadline day instead of three days early. A restricted gift gets deposited and nobody codes it correctly.

Each of those is cheap in isolation. Stacked together, they become the problems that put a nonprofit on a funder's watch list:

- A late or incomplete Form 990, which brings daily IRS penalties.
- Three missed annual filings in a row, which revokes tax-exempt status automatically.
- Restricted funds spent on general operations because no one tracked the restriction.
- A funder asking for an audit and finding the records are not ready.

The revocation rule is the one that surprises people. The IRS does not send a warning shot after the second year. If an organization does not file for three consecutive years, exempt status ends. Getting it back means a new application and, often, months of explaining. A leader running on four hours of sleep and a half-finished to-do list is exactly the person who lets a filing slide twice and then loses track of the third.

The [SBA's guidance on managing your business](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business) makes the same point from the operations side: sustainable organizations build routines that do not depend on one person's heroics. Much of it carries over to a nonprofit's back office.

## What burnout looks like on a nonprofit's books

You do not need a diagnosis to spot it. The financial symptoms tend to show up before the personal ones are admitted.

| What you notice | What is often behind it | What to do about it |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly financials arrive after the board meeting | The leader is the only reviewer and keeps pushing it | Set a fixed monthly close date and a second reviewer |
| Grant reports go in on the last day | Too many roles sit with one person | Calendar internal deadlines a week ahead of the funder's |
| Cash gets tight in the same months every year | No forecast, so seasonal gaps are a surprise | Build a 12-month cash forecast and update it quarterly |
| Nobody can say how much is restricted | Gifts are coded in a hurry | Track restrictions by fund and review them monthly |
| Only one person knows the passwords and processes | The bookkeeper left, or was never backed up | Write a short handoff document and name a backup |

If three of those rows sound familiar, that is not a personal failing. It is a workload problem, and workload problems have workable fixes.

## A scenario we see often in Laramie

Picture a Laramie nonprofit with a $450,000 annual budget, two full-time staff and a volunteer board. The part-time bookkeeper moves away in February. The executive director, who is already writing two grant applications, takes over data entry "just until we hire someone."

By May, no one has posted the March or April bank activity. The Form 990 is due May 15 for a calendar-year organization, and nobody has filed Form 8868 to extend it. An extension is automatic and adds six months, but only if someone files it. A funder emails to ask for year-to-date financials before releasing the second half of a grant, and the honest answer is that they do not exist yet.

Nothing in that story is about incompetence. It is about one person holding five jobs. A bookkeeper leaving is one of the most common moments we get a call from a nonprofit, and it is better to call before the backlog builds than after.

## Self-care as internal control

Accountants think about internal controls: the checks that keep one person's error or absence from damaging the organization. Rest belongs in that category.

A leader who takes a real week off has to document what they do. Someone else has to know where the bank login lives. A backup signer has to exist. Those are the same safeguards an auditor looks for, and you get them as a side effect of taking a vacation.

The reverse is also true. If the organization cannot function for five days without you, that is a control weakness. It is a risk to the board and to your funders, and it deserves the same attention as a missing approval signature.

So here is the reframe we offer our clients. Protecting your attention, sleep and time away is not a reward for finishing the work. It is part of the cost of doing the work accurately.

## Habits that protect your calendar and your cash

None of these require a retreat or a new app. They require a decision and a standing reminder.

**Put every compliance date on a shared calendar.** That means the Form 990 or 990-T due date, the extension date, state filings, and grant report deadlines. Share the calendar with your board chair and treasurer so the dates do not live only in your head.

**Hold a 45-minute monthly financial review.** Meet with the treasurer, look at the statements, and ask three questions: What changed, what is restricted, and what is coming due? Short and regular beats long and rare.

**Write the handoff before you need it.** One page covering who pays bills, how deposits are coded, where the records are kept, and who to call. If the bookkeeper or finance lead leaves, the next person starts from paper instead of guesswork.

**Cut the recurring noise.** Every unused software seat and forgotten subscription is a small decision you keep paying for. Our post on [subscription creep at a Laramie nonprofit](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep) walks through a quick audit that gives back both dollars and mental space.

**Block time off around the grant cycle, not in spite of it.** Look at when your big deadlines fall and schedule real time away in the quiet weeks. Tell the board the dates in January. A planned absence is a plan. An unplanned collapse in November is a crisis.

## Boards share the responsibility

Board members often admire a leader who never takes a day off. That admiration is misplaced. A nonprofit whose director cannot step away is carrying concentration risk, and the board is responsible for managing it.

A few practical steps for board chairs and treasurers:

- Ask for the monthly financials by a set date, and ask the question "Who covers if you are out?" once a year.
- Approve a modest budget line for outside help during busy periods, such as grant season or the annual audit.
- Make it normal for staff to use vacation time. Leaders watch what the board does, not what the handbook says.

When turnover hits the board itself, the same logic applies. New treasurers inherit whatever process exists. If the process is "the director remembers," the new treasurer has nothing to review.

## Common questions

**Is self-care for business owners really a financial issue?**

Yes. Fatigue raises the odds of missed deadlines, miscoded transactions and rushed spending decisions, and each one has a cost. For a nonprofit, the worst case is lost tax-exempt status after three years of unfiled returns. Rest protects the judgment that keeps the organization compliant and funded.

**What are the early signs of business owner burnout in a nonprofit?**

Look for financial statements that arrive late, grant reports filed on deadline day, and one person who holds every login and process. These show up in the books before they show up in conversation. If two or three apply, it is time to redistribute work or bring in outside help.

**How can a nonprofit leader take time off without risking compliance?**

Put every filing and grant deadline on a shared calendar, name a backup signer, and write a one-page handoff covering bill payment, deposits and records. Then schedule time away in a quiet stretch. A CPA can cover Form 990 preparation and monthly reviews while you are out.
