A board votes on an investment mix the year a bequest or a big gift arrives, writes it into the minutes, and doesn't look at it again until an auditor or funder asks. Five years later the portfolio looks nothing like the one the board approved, and nobody voted for the change. That is the quiet cost of "set it and forget it" for a non-profit. The money is still invested. It just isn't invested the way the board intended, and the organization is carrying risk it never agreed to.

A balanced investment portfolio isn't a one-time decision. It is a short, repeating routine that a volunteer board can keep. Here is what that routine looks like for Laramie non-profits.

## Drift is a decision your board never made

Markets don't leave your allocation alone. Whichever asset class has been winning slowly takes over the portfolio, and the mix you chose becomes a different mix.

Here is a simple example. Say a Laramie non-profit holds a $500,000 reserve fund, split 60 percent stocks and 40 percent bonds, which is $300,000 and $200,000. Stocks rise 25 percent in a strong year while bonds stay flat.

| | Start | After a strong year | After rebalancing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stocks | $300,000 (60%) | $375,000 (65%) | $345,000 (60%) |
| Bonds | $200,000 (40%) | $200,000 (35%) | $230,000 (40%) |
| Total | $500,000 | $575,000 | $575,000 |

A five-point drift looks harmless. Now suppose stocks then fall 30 percent. The drifted portfolio drops from $575,000 to about $462,500, a loss of roughly 19.6 percent. The rebalanced portfolio drops to about $471,500, a loss of 18 percent. That gap is about $9,000, and it comes from one small sale of $30,000 in stocks.

The difference grows when the drift runs for years instead of one. Portfolio rebalancing is the habit of selling a little of what has run up and buying what has lagged, so the risk level stays where the board set it. It feels backwards in the moment, which is exactly why it needs a schedule instead of a mood.

## Start with the cash you will need, not the return you want

The most common mistake we see in non-profit investing is picking an allocation before answering a plainer question: when will we need this money?

A workable asset allocation strategy for a non-profit usually sorts funds into three buckets.

- **Operating cash.** Money for the next 3 to 6 months of expenses, held in cash or short-term instruments. If your organization spends $30,000 a month, that is $90,000 to $180,000 that should never depend on the stock market.
- **Board-designated reserves.** Money the board has set aside for a roof, a program expansion, or a lean year. The time horizon is one to five years, so the mix leans conservative.
- **Long-term or endowment funds.** Money meant to last. This is the only bucket where a heavier stock allocation makes sense, because it has years to recover from a bad stretch.

Grant timing matters here. A reimbursement-based grant can leave you waiting 60 to 90 days for cash you have already spent. If your operating bucket is thin, you end up selling investments at a bad moment to cover payroll for the month. Sorting the buckets first prevents that.

Also watch donor-restricted gifts. If a donor restricted a gift to a specific purpose or period, that money belongs in the shorter-term bucket regardless of how the board feels about market returns.

## Risk tolerance belongs to the organization, not the loudest board member

Individual investors can lean on gut feel. A non-profit can't, because the people in the room change. A treasurer who lived through 2008 may want almost no stocks. A newer member who started investing in 2020 may want almost nothing else. Neither is wrong, and neither should set policy.

Risk tolerance investing for an organization comes down to two questions:

1. How large a drop could we absorb before we cut programs or staff?
2. How long could we wait for a recovery without hurting the mission?

If an 18 percent decline in the reserve would force you to cancel a program, your real risk tolerance is lower than your enthusiasm during a bull market suggested. Write the answer into a one- or two-page investment policy statement. It should list:

- The target mix for each bucket, with an acceptable range (for example, stocks 55 to 65 percent)
- Who is authorized to make changes
- How often the portfolio is reviewed
- What triggers an unscheduled review

The policy outlasts any one board member. When the treasurer rotates off, the next person inherits a plan instead of a guess. This is also the document a funder or auditor will ask for first.

## Portfolio diversification has a tax and compliance side

Spreading money across stocks, bonds, and cash is the familiar version of portfolio diversification. For a non-profit, the less obvious part is making sure the holdings don't create filing problems.

**Unrelated business income.** Most interest, dividends, and capital gains are excluded from unrelated business income tax. But some investments are not. Partnership interests that pass through operating business income on a K-1, and investments that use borrowing, can produce unrelated business income. If gross unrelated business income reaches $1,000 in a year, the organization has to file Form 990-T. A board member who adds an alternative fund to "diversify" can create a new filing requirement and a tax bill nobody budgeted for. We prepare Form 990 and 990-T, so we like to see a fund's structure before the money goes in.

**Private foundations.** A private foundation owes an excise tax of 1.39 percent on net investment income. It also has to distribute roughly 5 percent of its average investment assets each year for charitable purposes. Those two rules shape how much return the foundation needs, and how much liquidity it has to keep on hand.

**Reporting.** Endowment and investment balances show up on your Form 990 and its schedules, and on the financial statements your board and funders read. Holdings that are hard to value or hard to explain make those reports harder to defend.

**State law.** Most states have adopted a version of the Uniform Prudent Management of Institutional Funds Act, which asks boards to manage investments with care and to consider diversification and the organization's purposes. Your attorney can confirm how it applies to your organization, but the practical point is simple: a written policy and a documented review schedule are your best evidence that the board did its job.

## A review schedule a volunteer board can keep

The goal is a routine light enough that it survives a busy season and a change in leadership. This is one workable version.

| When | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Quarterly | Compare current mix to policy ranges and rebalance if any bucket is outside its range | Treasurer or finance committee |
| Annually | Re-check the cash forecast, operating bucket size, and policy targets | Finance committee, with board approval |
| After a trigger event | Hold a short review if there is a large gift or bequest, a funder change, a leadership change, or a market swing of 15 percent or more | Finance committee |
| Before year-end | Confirm that holdings won't create 990-T exposure and that gains and losses are documented | CPA and treasurer |

Two habits make this work. First, rebalance with new money when you can. If a gift arrives, direct it to whichever bucket is under target, which avoids selling anything. Second, put the comparison on a single page: target, actual, and variance for each bucket. A board member with ten minutes should be able to read it and know whether anything needs a vote.

## Common Questions

**How often should a non-profit rebalance its investment portfolio?**

Most non-profits do well reviewing allocation quarterly and rebalancing when a bucket moves more than 5 percentage points outside its target range. Also review after a large gift, a leadership change, or a market swing of 15 percent or more. A fixed range prevents both neglect and overreaction.

**What is a balanced investment portfolio for a non-profit?**

It is a mix matched to when the money will be needed. Operating cash covering 3 to 6 months of expenses stays in cash, reserves lean conservative, and only long-term or endowment funds carry a heavier stock allocation. The board writes the target mix and ranges into an investment policy statement.

**Can investments cause a non-profit to owe tax?**

Yes. Partnership interests that pass through operating business income, and investments that use borrowed money, can create unrelated business income. Once gross unrelated business income reaches $1,000 in a year, the organization must file Form 990-T. Review any new fund's structure with your CPA before investing.

**Do we need an investment policy statement?**

It isn't required for every organization, but it is the strongest evidence that your board managed funds prudently. A one- to two-page statement covering target mix, ranges, authority, and review dates is enough for most Laramie non-profits.
