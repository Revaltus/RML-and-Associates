The conversation usually starts with two numbers: what you pay in rent now and what a mortgage "would be." Neither is the whole comparison. If you work for a Laramie nonprofit, serve on its board, or run it, the decision also touches grant-cycle job security, a salary that may not grow fast, and a down payment that is probably most of your savings.

This post walks through the math the way we would at a coffee meeting. It uses illustrative numbers, so swap in your own.

## Start with your personal financial statement

A personal financial statement is a one-page list of what you own and what you owe. Your organization's board sees the same idea every quarter as a statement of financial position. Buying a home changes that page more than almost any other decision you will make.

On the day you close, cash drops, a large asset appears, and a 30-year loan shows up on the other side. Your net worth does not change on day one, but your liquidity falls sharply. That matters if your position is funded by a grant that ends in 18 months.

The Federal Reserve's [Survey of Consumer Finances](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf20.pdf) shows why people buy. In the 2019 survey, median net worth for homeowners was roughly $255,000, against about $6,300 for renters. That gap is real, but part of it reflects who tends to buy: people with steadier income and more savings. It does not prove that any particular house will build wealth for any particular buyer.

So before you compare payments, write down three things:

- Cash you could put toward a purchase, and cash you must keep as an emergency reserve.
- Monthly take-home pay, and how much of it is stable.
- How long you realistically expect to stay in Laramie.

The third item drives the answer more than any of the others.

## Price the full cost of owning, not just the mortgage

The mortgage payment is the number people compare to rent, and it is the smallest part of the story. Here is an illustrative $300,000 home with 10 percent down, a 30-year loan at 6.75 percent, and a comparable rental at $1,500 a month. These are example figures, not a quote for any specific property.

| Monthly cost | Own | Rent |
|---|---|---|
| Principal and interest on $270,000 | $1,750 | n/a |
| Property tax (assumed) | $150 | n/a |
| Homeowner's insurance (assumed) | $125 | n/a |
| Maintenance at 1 percent of value per year | $250 | n/a |
| Rent | n/a | $1,500 |
| **Total** | **$2,275** | **$1,500** |

The gap is about $775 a month. Part of the principal portion of that payment is you paying yourself, because it builds equity. In year one, though, only about $2,900 of the $21,000 in annual payments goes to principal. The rest is interest.

Maintenance is the line people skip. A furnace in a Wyoming winter, a roof after a hailstorm, or a water heater that quits in January all come out of your pocket, not a landlord's.

## Count what the down payment would have earned elsewhere

The down payment and closing costs on that $300,000 home add up to roughly $39,000: $30,000 down plus about 3 percent in closing costs. That money has a job to do whether it sits in a house or somewhere else.

If it stays in a savings account or a conservative portfolio earning 4 percent, it produces about $1,560 in the first year. That is a real cost of buying even though it never shows up on a mortgage statement. If your organization's board has ever sorted reserves into buckets, you already understand the idea. The same discipline applies to household savings, and our post on [keeping a balanced investment portfolio on a schedule](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) covers how to think about it.

There is a flip side. If you would not invest the difference and would spend it instead, owning can work as forced savings. Be honest with yourself about which person you are. The math can only reflect the behavior you actually have.

## Run both sides over the years you plan to stay

Now put the pieces together. Using the same example, assume the home appreciates 3 percent a year, rent rises 3 percent a year, and selling costs run about 7 percent of the price.

| Five-year result | Buy | Rent |
|---|---|---|
| Cash paid out (payments, upkeep, upfront costs) | about $175,600 | about $95,600 |
| Net sale proceeds after loan payoff and selling costs | about $70,000 | n/a |
| Growth on the $39,000 kept invested at 4 percent | n/a | about $8,450 |
| **Net cost over five years** | **about $105,600** | **about $87,100** |

Under these assumptions, renting comes out roughly $18,500 ahead after five years. Run the same model at seven and ten years and the gap narrows each year, because the selling costs get spread over more time and more of each payment goes to principal. Change the assumptions and the answer moves. A 5 percent appreciation rate or a 6 percent mortgage rate helps buying. Flat prices or a surprise $12,000 roof helps renting.

That is the point of building it as a model. You are not trying to predict the market. You are finding the break-even year and asking whether you will plausibly still be in the house then.

If you do not have a spreadsheet handy, any rent vs buy calculator will give you a rough answer. Just check what it assumes about maintenance, selling costs, and investment returns, because many leave those out.

## What the mortgage interest deduction does and does not do

Homeowners often count the mortgage interest deduction as a built-in discount. For many households it is smaller than expected. It only helps if your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction, which for a married couple has been above $29,000 in recent years. A household with a modest mortgage, state tax, and charitable gifts may land under that line and get no extra benefit at all.

IRS [Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505) explains which mortgage interest qualifies and the limits on how much debt counts. If you give generously to your own organization or others, your gifts count toward itemizing as well, so run the numbers both ways before you assume the deduction will matter.

It is also worth asking what your return looks like with and without a home. Our [personal income tax team](/personal-income-tax) can run both versions in a few minutes, and that beats guessing.

## Nonprofit paychecks add their own variables

A few details come up often in the Laramie nonprofit world, and they change the math.

**Grant-funded roles.** If your salary depends on a two- or three-year grant, a 30-year loan carries more risk than the same loan for someone with a permanent position. Lenders will look at your income history. You should look at your runway: how many months of housing costs you could cover if the grant did not renew?

**Student loans and forgiveness programs.** If you are on an income-driven repayment plan or working toward public service loan forgiveness, a larger mortgage can change how lenders see your debt-to-income ratio. Check your repayment status before you apply, not after.

**Board service and turnover.** Executive directors and program staff move more often than the 30-year loan assumes. If a board turnover or a new funder could send you to another city within five years, the five-year table above is your table, not the ten-year one.

**Variable income from side work.** Many people in the sector also consult, teach, or freelance. Lenders typically want a two-year history of that income. Keep clean records now and you will have a stronger application later.

## When the organization itself is the one deciding

The same question comes up for the nonprofit. Your organization may be paying rent on an office or program space and wondering whether a building would be better. The framework is identical, with a few extra layers.

A building turns cash reserves into a fixed asset, which can be hard to reverse quickly. A funder may restrict how grant money can be spent on property. The board should model maintenance, insurance, and a capital reserve for the roof and the boiler. It should also check whether any property tax exemption applies to the organization before assuming it does.

Read the board package the way a funder would. Does the statement of financial position still show enough unrestricted cash after the purchase? If not, renting longer may be the more responsible answer. ## Common Questions

**Is renting better than buying?**

Neither is better in general. Renting tends to win when you expect to stay fewer than five to seven years, your income is uncertain, or your savings would be drained by the down payment. Buying tends to win with a longer horizon and stable income. The break-even year in your own model decides it.

**What is the real cost of buying vs renting a home?**

Owning costs more than the mortgage. Add property tax, insurance, about 1 percent of the home's value per year for maintenance, closing costs near 3 percent, and roughly 7 percent to sell. Then subtract the equity you build. Renting costs the rent plus whatever your down payment earns elsewhere.

**Does the mortgage interest deduction make buying cheaper?**

Only if your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction. Many households with modest mortgages never cross that line. Check [IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505) for what qualifies, then compare your itemized total to the standard deduction before you count on the savings.
